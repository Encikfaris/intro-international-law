Your mission

The National Diplomatic World Simulation

One connected assignment across the whole semester. Two stages. Ten student-built nations. One international crisis. And you in the middle of it, deciding what the world should do.

Mission Briefing

You don't get handed a country. You build one.

First, you and your team create a fictional nation from scratch — its name, its flag, its capital, its government, its leader, its national interests. Everything is yours to design. Then the lecturer drops a crisis on the world: State Alpha has used military force against State Delta, claiming self-defence. Delta calls it aggression. The international community is called to an emergency assembly.

Now your country has to take a side. You'll research the law, write a full diplomatic position paper, deliver an opening statement, negotiate with other delegations, and vote on two competing resolutions. You are assessed on your legal reasoning — not on whether you agree with any real government.

The crisis

Alpha strikes Delta. The world reacts.

In the fictional world, tensions between State Alpha and State Delta have been building for years. Then Alpha launches military operations. Delta calls it aggression. Everything changes.

🔵
State Alpha

"We were defending ourselves."

Alpha claims Delta carried out or supported actions that threatened Alpha's forces and regional security. It says its military response was a lawful exercise of the right to self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter.

vs
🔴
State Delta

"This is aggression."

Delta rejects Alpha's justification entirely. It argues Alpha has violated its territorial sovereignty and the prohibition on the use of force — and that Alpha's actions amount to an act of aggression under international law.

Your job: work out whether Alpha's use of force was lawful — and decide what the international community should do about it. Both resolutions are on the table. Neither one is the "correct" answer. You have to choose, argue, and defend.

The 14-week journey

What you'll actually explore

Every week builds toward the simulation. By the time you sit in that assembly, you'll have the full legal toolkit you need — and you'll know exactly how to use it.

Week 01

Nature & Development of International Law

"Wait — who actually makes the rules?"

Where did international law come from, and why does it exist? You'll look at whether it's really "law" when there's no world police — and meet jus cogens, the handful of rules so fundamental that no country can ever opt out of them.

Week 02

Primary Sources of International Law

"Where do you find the law?"

Treaties. Customary law. General principles. You'll learn how a rule becomes binding in the first place — sometimes even when nobody signed anything.

Week 03

Secondary Sources & Subsidiary Means

"Whose opinion actually counts?"

Judges and scholars don't make the law — but they shape how it's read. You'll learn the difference between what's binding and what's merely persuasive.

Week 04

Municipal Law & International Law

"What if your country's law says something else?"

What happens when a treaty says one thing and your national law says another? You'll dig into the doctrines of incorporation and transformation, and how courts untangle the mess.

Week 05

Personality & Recognition

"How does a place become a country?"

Territory, government, population, capacity — the checklist for statehood. And why some places are countries to some states and not to others. Essential for the simulation.

Week 06

International Organisations

"The clubs countries join."

The UN isn't just a talk shop. You'll see how international organisations have legal personality, real powers, and real limits — and how they act on the world stage.

Week 07

State Responsibility

"When a country breaks the law."

What counts as an internationally wrongful act? When is a state responsible for what its officials do? And what happens after — who pays, and how?

Week 08

Law of Treaties & Diplomatic Relations

"The art of the deal — and the diplomat's shield."

How treaties are made, broken and ended. Why diplomats can't be arrested. The rules that keep international business running even when things get ugly.

Week 09

State Territory & Territorial Jurisdiction

"Whose ground is it anyway?"

Land, air, sea. How countries claim territory, defend it, and fight over it — using law, not just force. Disputes here have shaped the modern world.

Week 10

Jurisdiction & Competence of Courts

"How far can a country reach?"

Can a country arrest someone on the other side of the world? You'll look at extraterritorial reach, universal jurisdiction, and state immunity — the rules that decide who can be tried, and where.

Week 11

Use of Force & Self-Defence

"When is war actually legal?"

The big one. The prohibition on force, the UN Charter framework, Article 51, individual and collective self-defence — and the fine line between defence and aggression. This is the law at the heart of your simulation.

Week 12

Private International Law: Conflict of Laws

"When two countries' laws collide."

Not every dispute is between states. Sometimes it's two people, two countries, two sets of rules — and someone has to decide which one wins. You'll learn how connecting factors, domicile and public policy sort it out.

Week 13

Private International Law: Choice of Law

"Whose law applies here?"

Contracts, torts, marriage — how courts decide which country's law governs a private dispute. Express choice, implied choice, and what happens when the parties never agreed.

Week 14

Recognition & Enforcement of Foreign Judgements

"You won. Now can you actually collect?"

A judgment means nothing if you can't enforce it. You'll see how courts recognise and enforce decisions from other countries — and how arbitral awards get turned into real outcomes.

How you're assessed

One assignment. Three ways to show what you know.

The simulation is assessed across writing, speaking and a test — so your legal reasoning gets tested from every angle.

20% Presentation — Diplomatic Role Simulation
20% Assignment — Case Study / Position Paper
10% Test

You are the government.

Your task isn't to find the lecturer's preferred answer. It's to make the best legal and diplomatic decision for your country — and defend it in front of everyone.
